Open any one. Each gives the plain answer, three things worth knowing, and how the claim is filed.

the chip chain Nvidia designs the world’s AI chips. Nvidia does not build them. read

Outside foundries. Nvidia’s own annual report says so.

The company holding most of the world’s AI compute owns no fabs at all.

  • Nvidia designs. Over 60 percent of global AI compute runs on its chips.
  • TSMC manufactures. 77 percent of its wafer revenue comes from the most advanced nodes.
  • ASML makes the machine. The only maker on earth of the lithography TSMC needs.

Arguments about AI usually name the labs. The narrowest point in the whole system is a Dutch equipment maker most people have never heard of. Each machine weighs about 180 tonnes and is roughly the size of a school bus.

who owns the labs Nobody will say who owns the AI labs. read

Never disclosed. Not by either company, not anywhere.

Tens of billions of dollars committed, and no public figure for what it bought.

  • Microsoft says 27 percent. Of OpenAI, on an as converted basis. Disclosed and quotable.
  • Amazon does not say. Billions committed, plus a multi gigawatt compute deal. No percentage.
  • Google does not say. Up to 40 billion dollars announced. No percentage.

Every claim about a lab’s independence is a claim about a structure the public cannot currently see. This is not an accusation. It is a description of how much visibility anyone outside actually has.

who will give you a number Almost nobody building AI will say how risky they think it is. read

Amodei said 25 percent. Most of the others decline outright.

The number travels far further than the people who would actually stand behind one.

  • Hassabis: too precise. A number “would imply a level of precision that is not there.”
  • Bengio: poll instead. Sets out a framework, then says the figures should come from many experts.
  • Yudkowsky: drop the metric. And he is at the high end. He argues the number itself is the problem.

When someone quotes you a probability, ask what outcome they mean, by when, and whether they are counting the chance it never happens. Most quoted numbers do not survive those three questions. The surveys are more informative than the quotes.

Yoshua Bengio Helped invent modern AI. Now works on the risks. read

None. The most authoritative report in the field declines to give a number.

Backed by more than thirty governments, written by over a hundred experts, and it will not put a figure on it.

  • Set out the structure. His 2023 FAQ builds a framework, then leaves the probabilities blank.
  • Poll, do not guess. He proposes the numbers come from many experts rather than from him.
  • Threshold, not forecast. His argument is that even a small chance of an unrecoverable outcome warrants acting.

He is the clearest example that concern and rigour are not opposites. He refuses the number and still argues for action. If you want one person to read on this subject rather than one prediction to quote, it is probably him.

someone’s estimate Bengio, FAQ on Catastrophic AI Risks, 2023;
International AI Safety Report 2026
Yann LeCun Won the same prize as Bengio. Reached the opposite conclusion about AI. read

He has not given one. He rejects the framing, not just the figure.

Which makes him harder to argue with than a low number would be, not easier.

  • Capability is overstated. He argues current systems are much further from general ability than claimed.
  • Design is a choice. Systems get the objectives we build into them; danger is not automatic.
  • The debate is distorted. He thinks the risk case rests on assumptions rarely made explicit.

Because the disagreement is real and it is internal. Three people who built the field, one prize, three positions. Any account of this subject that only quotes one side of that is selling you something.

someone’s estimate LeCun interview, TIME, 13 February 2024
Bender and Hanna Their objection to AI doom is not about the odds. read

It diverts attention from harms already happening now.

A claim about where attention should go, not a forecast about the future.

  • Harms already counted. Deployed systems doing measurable damage to real people today.
  • Power, not machines. Their focus is on who owns the systems rather than what the systems might become.
  • Hype as strategy. They argue overstated capability claims serve the companies making them.

One preregistered experiment found existential risk narratives did not reduce concern for immediate harms, at least in individual attitudes. That is one study on one question. The argument about institutional attention is not settled by it.
